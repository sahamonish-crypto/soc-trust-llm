# 1. Introduction

**Draft status:** PRE-RESULTS / PRE-GATE-7  
**Citation notation:** `[P#]` refers to the numbered primary-paper corpus and will be converted to the target journal's citation style later.

Security Operations Centers (SOCs) must identify and investigate potentially malicious activity from large, heterogeneous streams of security alerts. High alert volumes, false positives, time pressure, and limited analyst capacity can contribute to alert fatigue and delayed investigation, motivating continued research on automated and AI-assisted triage [P3, P6, P7]. Large language models (LLMs) have consequently been investigated for tasks including security-alert classification, prioritisation, log interpretation, incident analysis, and analyst-facing decision support [P1, P2, P26, P27, P28, P31]. These capabilities make LLMs promising components of SOC workflows, but technical capability alone does not establish that analysts will use their recommendations appropriately.

Recent work has already moved beyond purely autonomous cybersecurity automation. Empirical and conceptual studies examine human–AI collaboration in SOCs, trusted autonomy, analyst oversight, and trust-aware operational models [P1, P3, P4, P5]. These studies make clear that the relevant research question is not whether humans should simply trust AI more. Human–automation research instead emphasizes **appropriate reliance**: reliance should correspond to the actual capability and limitations of the automated system [P22]. Excessive reliance can produce misuse or automation bias, whereas insufficient reliance can cause potentially useful decision support to be ignored. Cybersecurity-specific human-factors research similarly identifies automation bias, confirmation bias, workload, and trust as important considerations when analysts interact with AI-supported tools [P23].

This distinction is particularly important for LLM-based decision support because a fluent recommendation does not necessarily communicate how likely that recommendation is to be correct. LLM confidence estimation and calibration therefore form a separate technical problem from generating the recommendation itself [P30, P33, P34, P35]. Prior work has explored verbalized confidence, sampling-based uncertainty, and consistency across multiple generations. Sample-consistency approaches are especially relevant to black-box or API-accessed models because repeated model outputs can provide an observable uncertainty signal without requiring direct access to model internals [P34]. Calibration research also shows why confidence quality should be evaluated quantitatively rather than assumed from the model's wording, using measures such as the Brier score and reliability-based analyses [P33, P34, P35].

A second challenge is that technically calibrated confidence does not automatically guarantee calibrated human reliance. Human–AI decision-making studies outside the SOC context show that uncertainty communication, perceived model capability, and presentation choices can influence trust and reliance behavior [P8, P9, P10, P29]. Consequently, evaluating an uncertainty estimator only as a machine-learning metric leaves an important question unanswered: whether a confidence signal that performs adequately on held-out data helps human decision-makers discriminate when an AI recommendation should or should not be followed. This behavioral question is distinct from subjective trust. In this study, **trust** is treated as an attitude toward the AI system, whereas **reliance** is treated as observable decision behavior [P22].

Existing SOC research provides important evidence that LLMs can support analyst workflows and that human oversight remains central [P1–P5]. Existing calibration research provides techniques for estimating and evaluating LLM uncertainty [P30, P33–P35]. Existing human–AI research provides theories and measures for studying over-reliance, under-reliance, and appropriate trust [P8–P10, P22, P29]. However, these bodies of work do not by themselves establish whether a **prospectively selected and independently evaluated confidence signal** can support appropriate analyst reliance during SOC alert triage. This motivates a study design that treats technical calibration and human evaluation as separate stages rather than tuning confidence presentation directly on participant outcomes.

Accordingly, this work develops a staged evaluation pipeline for LLM-assisted SOC triage. First, candidate LLM configurations are compared under a predefined model-selection procedure. Second, uncertainty estimators and post-hoc calibration mappings are evaluated on held-out development data using prospectively specified criteria, with the selected method frozen before evaluation on an untouched calibration-EVAL set. Third, only if the predefined EVAL safeguard is satisfied will the project proceed to participant-facing evaluation of the resulting decision-support intervention. Calibration development data, calibration-EVAL data, human-study items, and the external TEST set are kept logically separate to reduce information leakage and post-hoc adaptation.

The technical protocol further separates three concepts that are often conflated: the **uncertainty estimator**, which produces a raw confidence signal; the **calibration mapping**, which may transform that signal using held-out labeled data; and the **participant-facing display**, which communicates the resulting information to users. The first two are evaluated quantitatively before any final human-study confidence intervention is frozen. This separation allows the project to determine whether a candidate confidence mechanism has useful discrimination and calibration properties before asking whether its presentation changes analyst behavior.

The intended contribution is therefore not a claim that LLM alert triage, human–AI SOC collaboration, retrieval-augmented incident analysis, uncertainty calibration, or automation-bias research is itself new. Rather, the study is designed to connect these established research areas through a prospectively controlled pipeline that evaluates technical confidence quality before examining its relationship with human reliance in SOC triage. The exact participant-facing intervention, final human-study contrasts, participant population, sample size, trial count, and confirmatory human-study analysis remain intentionally unfrozen until the technical Gate 6 evaluation is complete.
